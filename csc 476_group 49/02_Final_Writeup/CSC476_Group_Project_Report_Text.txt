ABSTRACT
Maintenance information management requires a connection between reported faults, 
assigned work and documented outcomes. This project develops a web-based prototype for 
help desk management, project tracking and field reporting intended for the Maintenance 
Department, University of Ibadan. It addresses the need to demonstrate these related 
functions in a common application. The initial requirements are literature-informed; the 
university’s existing procedures have not been investigated through interviews or direct 
observation.
An iterative development approach was used to translate provisional requirements into a 
modular application and relational database. Python and Flask provide request processing, 
SQLite stores records, and Jinja2, HTML and CSS provide server-rendered interfaces. The 
implementation includes users, tickets, ticket updates, projects, tasks and field reports. 
Evaluation used an isolated test database and synthetic demonstration records, with 
browser navigation and screenshot capture through Playwright and Chromium.
The prototype supports core ticket operations, task assignment and progress updates, 
structured field reports and summary views. The verification test run completed with 17 
passing tests, and eight genuine application screenshots were captured. These results 
demonstrate selected functions under test conditions, not institutional effectiveness. 
Record-level authorisation, validation, administrative functions and workflow controls 
remain incomplete. Stakeholder validation, security improvements and broader evaluation 
are recommended before any operational deployment.

CHAPTER ONE: INTRODUCTION
1.1 Preamble
The operation of a university depends partly on the availability and condition of its 
physical facilities. Buildings, electrical installations, water systems and equipment support 
teaching, research and administrative activities. When a fault occurs, maintenance work 
must be coordinated through a process that records the problem, identifies its location, 
assigns responsibility and documents the action taken.
Maintenance management therefore involves both technical work and the information 
required to organise that work. A repair may be completed successfully, but without an 
adequate record, supervisors may be unable to determine when it occurred, who performed 
it or whether follow-up action is needed. The U.S. Department of Energy (2010) identifies 
work-order tracking and retrievable maintenance history as important functions of 
computerised maintenance management systems.
Three information processes are central to this project. Help desk management provides a 
structured channel for receiving and tracking requests. Project tracking organises larger 
activities into tasks with responsibilities and deadlines. Field reporting records the findings 
and actions associated with work carried out at a particular location. Connecting these 
processes can provide a clearer view of a maintenance activity from its initial report to its 
documented outcome.
This project develops a web-based prototype intended for the Maintenance Department, 
University of Ibadan. The initial requirements are derived from selected literature and 
illustrative scenarios, rather than interviews or direct observation. The work consequently 
demonstrates a proposed software solution without claiming that the university's present 
procedures have been investigated or that operational improvements have been measured.
1.2 Statement of Problems
A maintenance information system needs to preserve the relationship between a reported 
fault, the person assigned to address it and the record of the work performed. When 
requests, assignments and technical reports are held separately, obtaining a complete 
history can require consultation of several records. A request register alone may not

support multi-task projects, while a project tracker may not capture technical findings from 
field work.
The problem addressed by this project is the need to demonstrate a common application 
that records requests, allocates work, tracks project tasks and retains structured field 
reports. Specifically, the proposed solution addresses request identification, responsibility 
assignment, progress visibility, technical documentation and summary reporting.
These are literature-informed information-management needs, not verified findings about 
the University of Ibadan. The extent to which they match the department's current 
difficulties must be established through authorised consultation before deployment.
1.3 Aim and Objectives
The aim is to design, implement and evaluate a web-based prototype for the automation of 
help desk management, project tracking and field reporting for the Maintenance 
Department, University of Ibadan.
The specific objectives are to:
1. Identify and document literature-informed functional and non-functional 
requirements.
2. Design the architecture, database and user interfaces for the proposed maintenance 
workflow.
3. Implement ticket submission, assignment, commenting and status tracking.
4. Implement project and task creation, assignment, deadlines and progress recording.
5. Implement structured field reporting with optional links to maintenance tickets.
6. Provide authentication, appropriate access controls and summary reporting.
7. Evaluate selected functions through documented tests using synthetic records.
1.4 Justification
The project is justified as an applied software-development exercise that brings related 
maintenance records into one application. A shared interface can make it easier to locate 
request details, identify assigned personnel and review associated technical reports. The 
proposed ticket identifiers and update records support traceability, while task records 
provide a basis for monitoring planned activities.

The use of widely available development tools makes the prototype reproducible within a 
student project environment. Its source code can be examined, revised and tested without 
depending on a proprietary application platform. However, this does not establish the total 
cost of institutional operation: hosting, support, training, security and maintenance would 
still require resources.
The project contributes a working application together with its design documentation and 
test evidence. Reduced response times, improved productivity and greater satisfaction are 
possible outcomes for later investigation, not findings of this project.
1.5 Scope of Study
The scope covers a single-department prototype with three principal modules: help desk 
ticketing, project tracking and field reporting. Supporting facilities include user 
authentication, account creation, dashboard summaries and a ticket-report filter.
The proposed roles are Administrator, Supervisor, Technician and Staff. They are design 
assumptions rather than confirmed university job classifications. Staff submit tickets; 
supervisors coordinate work; technicians update activities and submit reports; 
administrators maintain account access.
The prototype does not include payroll, procurement, contractor billing, comprehensive 
inventory, sensor integration or a native mobile application. It does not provide offline 
operation or integration with university systems. Tickets and projects currently remain 
separate record types; field reports can link to tickets but not directly to project tasks.
Evaluation is limited to selected automated checks and browser inspection using synthetic 
information. There has been no institutional deployment, participant usability study or 
operational performance comparison.
1.6 Methodology
Development proceeded in stages, with testing and revision after each stage. The reviewed 
literature provided the initial requirements, which were translated into application modules, 
database models and browser pages. Authentication and ticketing were developed 
alongside project tracking, field reporting and reporting facilities. Automated tests were 
then used to check selected operations and identify defects for correction.

CHAPTER TWO: REVIEW OF RELEVANT 
LITERATURE
2.1 Introduction and Review Approach
This chapter presents a focused review of selected literature relevant to ticketing, 
maintenance information management and project monitoring. It is not a systematic review 
of all available research. Sources were selected for their direct relevance to the proposed 
functions and the availability of identifiable publication details.
The review uses two academic studies and official maintenance-management guidance. 
This is a limited literature base and should be expanded if the lecturer requires broader 
coverage. Source claims are distinguished from project design choices; findings from other 
organisations are not treated as evidence about the University of Ibadan.
2.2 Conceptual Review
2.2.1 Maintenance management and CMMS
The U.S. Department of Energy (2010) describes a computerised maintenance 
management system as software supporting the management and tracking of operations 
and maintenance activities. Capabilities can include work-order generation, prioritisation, 
maintenance history, scheduled activities and reporting. More extensive installations may 
also provide inventory control, labour-cost tracking and integration with other systems.
The guide emphasises assessing needs rather than choosing software solely by feature 
count. It also identifies training and sustained commitment as implementation concerns. 
For this project, that guidance supports a limited initial scope and the requirement for 
future consultation with maintenance personnel.
2.2.2 Help desk ticketing
A ticket is the proposed system's electronic representation of an individual request. Its 
identifier, description, category, priority, status and assignment allow the request to remain 
recognisable during handling. Wonohardjo, Putra and Yossy (2022) demonstrate a web-
based helpdesk approach in an organisational setting and identify maintainability and 
platform dependence as design concerns.

CSC 476  |  MAINTENANCE INFORMATION SYSTEM  |  REVIEW DRAFT
6
The present prototype adopts structured request recording and a basic update history. Its 
ticketing module should not be confused with a fully validated service-management 
process: approval rules and permitted status transitions still require refinement.
2.2.3 Project tracking
Project tracking organises work into activities whose responsibilities and progress can be 
monitored. Hambali, Anisyah and Arianti (2024) describe a web-based project 
management application with task scheduling, notifications and expenditure monitoring. 
Their study illustrates the use of tasks and schedules to make ongoing work visible.
The present project adopts task assignment, deadlines and progress recording, but excludes 
financial controls and automated notifications. Maintenance requirements cannot be 
inferred directly from software-team procedures; the selected concepts require adaptation 
and user validation.
2.2.4 Field reporting
In this project, a field report is a structured account of a site activity, containing the 
location, equipment, findings, action taken and date. These fields are design choices 
intended to support the retrievable maintenance history described by the U.S. Department 
of Energy (2010).
A field report differs from a status update. Status indicates the stage of work, while the 
report explains what was found and done. A completed label alone does not document the 
technical basis for closing a request.
2.3 Technical Framework
The design combines three complementary ideas: work-order traceability, task-based 
monitoring and retrievable technical history. Work-order traceability informs the ticket and 
update records. Task-based monitoring informs project and task entities. Retrievable 
history informs the linkage between tickets and field reports.
Role separation is an additional design requirement. The application should distinguish 
requesting, executing, supervising and administering activities. These responsibilities must 
be enforced through route and record-level checks rather than relying on hidden interface 
buttons or role labels alone.

The progress indicator is deliberately simple. The prototype averages the percentages of a 
project's tasks and returns zero for a project with no tasks. This assumes equal task 
importance and is not a weighted measure of effort, expenditure or physical completion.
2.4 Review of Related Studies and Guidance
2.4.1 Wonohardjo, Putra and Yossy (2022)
The authors developed a web-based helpdesk ticketing system for PT DENSO Indonesia. 
Their published abstract describes limitations of the previous IBM Lotus Notes-based 
arrangement, including restricted features, platform dependence and difficulty extending 
the source code. The replacement was developed using Extreme Programming and UML 
modelling.
The authors report an 86% completion rate in a problem-reporting scenario involving 15 
participants. This provides evidence from their evaluation context, not from the present 
project. Its main relevance is the practical demonstration of web-based request handling 
and the importance of evaluating user tasks. It does not establish the maintenance 
requirements of the University of Ibadan or the usability of this prototype.
2.4.2 Hambali, Anisyah and Arianti (2024)
This study describes a project management application for an ICT company. The authors 
used a waterfall approach and black-box testing. The described functions include task 
scheduling, project monitoring, notifications and comparisons between budget and 
expenditure.
The study informs the project's task-oriented design. Assignments and progress values can 
make work visible to a coordinator, but the context differs from physical maintenance. The 
present prototype therefore adopts selected scheduling and monitoring ideas without 
claiming to reproduce the complete system or its outcomes.
2.4.3 U.S. Department of Energy (2010)
The Operations and Maintenance Best Practices Guide provides technical guidance rather 
than an experimental evaluation of this project. Its CMMS chapter discusses needs 
assessment, capabilities, implementation risks and selection considerations. It identifies 
historical records and work-order tracking as useful functions while warning that training 
and continuing commitment are important.

This guidance prevents feature implementation from being equated with successful 
organisational adoption. The current software tests demonstrate selected behaviour; they 
do not demonstrate that staff have been trained, that the institution accepts the workflow or 
that records will be maintained consistently.

2.6 Identified Practical Gap

CHAPTER THREE: METHODOLOGY
3.1 Development Approach and Requirements Sources
The project used iterative implementation and testing. Requirements were derived from the 
selected literature and illustrative scenarios, including fault submission, technician 
assignment, project-task updates and site-report submission. No interviews, observations or 
inspection of university records were undertaken.
Iteration consisted of implementing functions, executing tests, examining failures and 
correcting code or test setup. It did not involve user-feedback sessions with maintenance 
personnel. The requirements are therefore provisional.
The demonstration dataset contains invented accounts and activities. Its purpose is to make 
the workflow testable and visible without using confidential operational data. It is not a 
sample from which university maintenance performance can be estimated.
3.2 Proposed Operational Model
A user submits a request. A supervisor reviews it and assigns a technician. Progress and 
comments are recorded against the ticket. A field report can then describe the findings and 
action taken. Larger activities can be recorded as projects with tasks, although a direct 
ticket-to-project link is not currently part of the schema.
Before adoption, the institution should confirm request categories, assignment authority, 
priority definitions, resolution and closure procedures, user visibility and reporting 
requirements. The official organisational designation of the maintenance unit should also 
be checked against the approved project title.

3.4 Non-Functional Requirements
Usability requires understandable labels, consistent navigation and clear feedback. The 
interface aims to meet these requirements, but participant testing has not been performed. 
Responsive styling is present; comprehensive mobile and cross-browser evaluation 
remains outstanding.
Security requires authentication, server-side permission checks, secure configuration, input 
validation and protection against forged state-changing requests. Password hashing and 
selected role checks exist. Complete record-level authorisation, cross-site request forgery 
protection and production security configuration remain unfinished.
Data integrity requires valid relationships and consistent updates. Model declarations alone 
do not prove enforcement: foreign-key behaviour, invalid references and transaction 
failures require explicit checks. Reliability also requires tested backup and restoration 
procedures, which have not yet been evaluated.
Performance targets should be defined against an expected workload. No concurrency or 
load evaluation has established the prototype's capacity. Maintainability is supported by 
separate modules and documented dependencies, but installation in a new environment 
must still be verified before submission or deployment.
3.5 Architecture
The application has three logical layers. The browser presents forms and tables. Flask

3.9 Progress Calculation
For a project with n tasks, summary progress is calculated as P = (p1 + p2 + ... + pn) / n, 
rounded to an integer. A project with no tasks returns zero. Each pi is a stored task 
percentage.
This calculation provides a simple interface indicator, not a precise measure of physical 
completion. If tasks vary in duration or effort, weighted progress would be more 
appropriate. Such weights should be agreed with users rather than invented for the report.

4.6 Defects Found During Development
The initial authentication failure arose because Flask-Login expected an identifier while 
the model used user_id. Defining get_id() to return that identifier as a string corrected the 
error.
A second issue arose in the test helper: it attempted to sign in as another user without 
ending the existing session. The login route correctly redirected already-authenticated 
users, preventing the intended test-account switch. The helper was corrected to log out 
first. This was a test-setup problem, not evidence that staff had supervisor permissions.
Ticket closure also initially failed because the code assigned the datetime.utcnow function 
to a date-time field rather than calling it. Adding parentheses supplied a datetime value, 
after which the test passed. The timestamp's naming and lifecycle semantics still need 
improvement, as discussed in Chapter Three.
4.7 Results and Discussion
The prototype demonstrates selected maintenance functions in a shared application. 
Database assertions provide evidence for record creation and updates; browser screenshots 
provide evidence that principal screens rendered. Together, they support feasibility within 
the limited test conditions.
They do not establish real-world effectiveness. No maintenance personnel participated, no 
baseline was measured, and no operational records were analysed. Claims of reduced 
delays, productivity improvements, cost savings or satisfaction would therefore be 
unsupported.

The modules share accounts and records but are not fully integrated across every activity. 
Tickets do not directly become projects, and field reports do not reference tasks. Reporting 
is basic, and the current progress calculation assumes equal task importance. These limits 
should guide the next development cycle rather than being hidden by a claim that all 
objectives are fully achieved.
4.8 Outstanding Issues and Evaluation Boundaries
Before operational use, the application needs record-level authorisation, scoped 
dashboards, comprehensive server validation, CSRF protection, secure secrets and session 
settings, password-reset and deactivation functions, defined status-transition rules and 
appropriate production hosting. The requirements file also needs controlled version 
management, and environment-variable instructions must be accurate: the current code 
reads process environment variables rather than automatically loading a .env file.
No load test, cross-browser programme, backup-restoration exercise, participant usability 
study or institutional user-acceptance test has been completed. The software should remain 
a local demonstration using synthetic records until these issues are addressed.

Core ticketing, project-task and field-report functions were implemented. Seventeen 
selected automated tests passed after identified defects were corrected, and eight real 
screenshots were captured. The evidence supports a functioning prototype, not production 
readiness or institutional effectiveness. Chapter Five draws conclusions within that
